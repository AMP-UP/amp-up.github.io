  • A seismic atlas of the stellar merger sky
    Nicholas Rui (Hubble @ Princeton)
    Abstract
    Stellar mergers are common but poorly understood processes. Owing to its sensitivity to internal stellar properties, asteroseismology is uniquely capable of discovering merger remnant stars that appear superficially normal. Red giants' rich pulsation spectra, strong coupling to core oscillations, and high rates of interaction make them optimal targets for seismic searches of past mergers. The hundreds of thousands of red giants measured by TESS and Roman will revolutionize these searches by building large enough seismic samples of merger remnants to probe the statistics of the mergers themselves. As a Hubble Fellow, I will model the seismic properties of a diverse zoo of red giant merger remnant stars. By comparing TESS and Roman catalogs to synthetic populations from next-generation binary population codes, I will extract the intrinsic rates of various types of stellar mergers.

  • Leveraging HST for a New Era of Precision NIR SN Ia Cosmology
    Justin Pierel (Einstein @ STScI)
    Abstract
    Near-infrared (NIR) observations from the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope will help to unravel the mysteries of dark energy. Type Ia supernovae (SN Ia) will be one of the principal anchors of the Roman cosmology program, requiring a model of SN Ia light curve evolution used to make corrections to the observed SN brightness and thereby infer a luminosity distance. Such corrections take into account, among other variables, that less luminous SN Ia are cooler and therefore appear redder. Unfortunately, this relationship is confounded by dust, which also produces fainter and redder objects, making it extremely difficult to correctly infer intrinsic SN Ia color, and thus luminosity. The consequence is one of the largest systematic uncertainties for SN Ia dark energy measurements today. Fortunately, Roman will be observing at rest-frame NIR wavelengths, where SN Ia are better standard candles and the impact of dust is extremely small. However, my work only recently trained the industry-standard model for SN Ia standardization (SALT3) at NIR wavelengths, so our understanding of NIR SN Ia cosmology is severely limited. As a Hubble Fellow, I will use my new model (SALT3-NIR) to make the first HST WFC3/IR-only measurement of the dark energy equation of state parameter, w. This work will significantly improve calibration and dust-related uncertainties compared to the optical and provide the first analysis of systematics/best practices for NIR SN Ia cosmology, which I will use to re-optimize the Roman survey for rest-frame optical+NIR.

  • The Role of Physical Processes and the Environment in Star Formation
    Dávid Guszejnov (Hubble @ Harvard CfA)
    Abstract
    Stars are the fundamental units of astrophysics and their formation is a key process that influences the evolution of galaxies, planets, and the development of life. Understanding how stars form is crucial even for seemingly unconnected fields of astronomy, as the interpretation of observed starlight from galaxies (even as a background) and other unresolved sources relies on our understanding of star formation (e.g., an assumed initial mass function of stars). There is no comprehensive theory of star formation, despite intense effort on both the theoretical and observational sides, due to the complex, non-linear physics involved and the enormous dynamic range of the problem. My goal is to understand the roles turbulence, magnetic fields, feedback and the local environment play in star formation, on scales from individual protostars to giant molecular clouds. I am building both analytical and numerical tools to answer questions like: What regulates star formation? What sets the characteristic mass of the stellar initial mass function (IMF)? Why are stars clustered? What sets the properties of binaries? How is star formation different in other galaxies?

  • Two Projects with Circumbinary Planets—Using the Planets to Solve Super-Earth Formation, and the Binaries to Empirically Constrain M-dwarfs
    David Martin (Sagan @ Ohio State)
    Abstract
    The Kepler mission has yielded two particularly revolutionary results. First, there is a plentiful abundance of Earth and super-Earth sized exoplanets. Second, we have uncovered planets orbiting two stars - circumbinary planets - which possess advantageous observational properties and provide a unique probe of planet formation. However, these two results remain mutually exclusive; no circumbinary planet has been discovered smaller than 3 Earth radii, owing to the insensitivity of searches. As a Hubble fellow I conduct a two-part project. First, I will create and implement sophisticated automated techniques to discover roughly 50 circumbinary Earths and super-Earths transiting Kepler binaries, and potentially more in TESS. Many will have habitable orbits comparable to the inner Solar System. Enhanced circumbinary transit probabilities mean that the stars will be amongst the brightest known to host transiting planets, making these pristine candidates for atmospheric characterisation. I will tap this previously hidden gold mine in the Kepler data, and provide a necessary development for TESS. The second part of my fellowship will be to interpret this plethora of new results to develop revolutionary comparative planetary formation models, and leave a legacy of both targets and techniques for future instruments such as JWST and PLATO.

  • Ultra-High Resolution Simulations of the Milky Way and its Satellites
    Coral Wheeler (Hubble @ Carnegie Observatories)
    Abstract
    The Local Group provides a Rosetta Stone for galaxy formation theory, linking cosmology and Galactic astronomy in a unique way. Many aspects of the spatial structure, star formation history, and satellites of the Milky Way (MW) are known to a precision that is unachievable in extragalactic systems, and we expect to soon have massive new observational datasets with which to test fundamental predictions of cosmological and galaxy formation models. Of course the MW and its satellites are not independent systems — the differences between the independent and interacting dwarf galaxies provide important clues to the relative importance of feedback and interactions with massive neighbors in regulating dwarf galaxy properties. I will extract analogues for a MW-mass galaxy and a pair of dwarfs at different masses from cosmological simulations ~2-4 Gyr before z=0 and re-simulate their interaction using orbital properties taken from current observations, focusing on two projects: 1) The effect of an encounter between dwarf galaxies and the MW on the star formation, kinematics and substructure of the interacting satellites and 2) The effect of the collision on the morphology and star formation history of the MW itself.

  • Subgiants: Models, Rotation, Convection, and Planets
    Jamie Tayar (Hubble @ University of Hawaii)
    Abstract
    Stellar models are the pillars of many areas of astrophysics and make a rich web of predictions for the properties and evolution of stars. While these models perform well for stars like our sun, important inconsistencies have been identified in other regimes. With the launch of the TESS satellite next year, in combination with Gaia data and ground based spectroscopy, there will finally be a large sample of stars with measured masses, radii, surface gravities, temperatures, metallicities, core and surface rotation rates, granulation and macroturbulence, which can be used to improve stellar models and therefore the inferences drawn from them. Specifically, I will use this sample to calibrate the masses and radii of stellar models, and better our understanding of stellar evolution, especially the physics of rotation and convection. This work will also improve the measured masses, radii, and ages of planets, provide insight into which stars are most likely to have detectable planets, and improve estimates of the planet distribution as a function of stellar mass and metallicity. In short, this work will improve both our understanding of our own cosmic origins, and our ability to study planetary systems outside our own.

    Andrew Vanderburg (Sagan @ UT Austin)
    Abstract
    The Kepler mission revolutionized our understanding of small exoplanets orbiting near their host stars, but Kepler only surveyed one small part of the sky.   • Dynamical detection and migration of multiple-body planetary systems
    Daniel Fabrycky (Michelson @ Harvard CfA)
    Abstract
    With the discovery of planets orbiting other stars came numerous examples of multiple-body systems: as of October 2006, 20 stars are known to host multiple planets and 31 planet-hosting stars are known to have at least one stellar companion. In our own planetary system, the planets only slightly perturb each other’s orbits because of their relatively small eccentricities and inclinations and their generous spacing. The planets of other stars, in contrast, generally have a strong dynamical coupling to one another. If one of the planets transits the host star, transit-timing measurements will exquisitely constrain the system’s parameters and may even permit the detection of additional planets. I will develop a theoretical and statistical framework to diagnose the dynamical state of planetary systems by such measurements. The long-term perturbation of a planet by a companion star can drive its orbit to high eccentricity; with the help of tidal dissipation the planet may migrate to become a hot Jupiter. I will make theoretical models of such evolution to constrain the histories of planets in binaries on the basis of their orbits observable by Navigator observatories SIM and TPF.

  • The Evolution of Neutral Gas from the Epoch of Reionization to the Present
    Jeremy Darling (Hubble @ UChicago)
    Abstract
    The gas phase of the universe, dominated by hydrogen, has undergone dramatic changes since radiation decoupled from matter. The first stars and quasars blew ionized bubbles in the neutral gas, and these Str ̈omgren spheres eventually overlapped to create a mostly ionized universe, leaving the surviving neutral hydrogen in overdense filaments tracing the early large scale structure. As density constrasts grew via gravitational instabilities, the neutral hydrogen played a key role in the evolution of galaxies. We propose to study the phase transition of the intergalactic medium during the epoch or reionization and the subsequent evolution of the remaining neutral hydrogen gas, including the connection to galaxy evolution, in a two-pronged effort. First, we will identify probes of the epoch or reionization for eventual absorption line studies, and second we will identify all damped Lyα absorption systems along many lines of sight from the present to the EOR — irrespective of dust content or redshift — and study the hosts of these systems across the epoch of galaxy evolution.
